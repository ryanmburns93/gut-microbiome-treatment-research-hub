## Overview

The *gut microbiota* is the community of microorganisms — mostly bacteria, but also archaea, fungi, and viruses — that live in the human digestive tract. The *gut microbiome* usually refers to those organisms together with their genes and the environment they live in.

## How many microbes are there?

For a long time it was repeated that the body holds about ten times more bacterial cells than human cells. That figure has been revised. In 2016, researchers estimated that a 70 kg reference adult has about 38 trillion bacteria and about 30 trillion human cells, so the two are of the same order of magnitude, a ratio of roughly one to one. Most of those bacteria live in the colon. [1]

The numbers vary from person to person, and the estimates carry real uncertainty. What holds up is that the community is very large and very diverse.

## Why it matters for treatment research

Researchers study the gut microbiome for several reasons:

- **Digestion and metabolism.** Gut microbes help break down food that our own enzymes cannot, and they produce substances that the body then absorbs and uses.
- **Immune system.** The gut is a major site where the immune system meets the outside world, and the microbial community is part of how it learns what to tolerate and what to fight.
- **Protection against infection.** A healthy community can make it harder for harmful organisms to take hold. When antibiotics disrupt that community, an infection such as *C. difficile* can return again and again. This is the case where restoring the community works best. See [fecal microbiota transplantation]({{ '/reading/fecal-microbiota-transplantation/' | relative_url }}).
- **The gut–brain connection.** The gut and brain communicate through nerves, the immune system, and chemical signals, and researchers are investigating whether gut microbes play a role in mood, behavior, and neurological conditions.

## What we know and don't know

The strongest evidence that changing the microbiome can treat disease is still narrow. Many studies find that people with a given condition have a different gut community from people without it, but that on its own does not show the difference causes the condition, or that changing it would help.
