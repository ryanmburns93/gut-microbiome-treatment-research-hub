## What it is

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) moves processed stool from a screened, healthy donor into the digestive tract of a patient. The aim is to restore a healthier community of gut microbes where the patient's own community has been disrupted.

Its best-known use is for *Clostridioides difficile* (*C. difficile*) infection. The antibiotics that treat this infection also disturb the normal gut community, and that disturbance is thought to leave room for *C. difficile* to return. In some people the infection comes back again and again. FMT is meant to break that cycle by re-establishing a more protective community.

## How it is delivered

Donor material can be given in several ways: through a colonoscopy, as an enema, through a tube that passes through the nose into the small intestine, or as capsules.

Two standardized products are now FDA-approved for recurrent *C. difficile* infection. Rebyota (approved in 2022) is given rectally. Vowst (approved in 2023) is taken by mouth as capsules. [5]

## Where the evidence is strongest

The clearest evidence is for **recurrent *C. difficile* infection**.

- In an early randomized trial, 81% of patients who received donor stool were cured after the first infusion, compared with 31% on standard vancomycin. The trial was small and was stopped after an interim analysis. [1]
- In ECOSPOR III, a placebo-controlled trial of 182 adults with repeated infections, 12% of those given the oral product (SER-109, now Vowst) had a recurrence within 8 weeks, compared with 40% on placebo. [2]
- The American Gastroenterological Association's 2024 guideline suggests fecal microbiota–based therapy for adults with recurrent infection, selectively, after standard antibiotics. It also suggests conventional FMT for hospitalized adults with severe or fulminant infection that is not responding to antibiotics. [3][4]

## Areas under investigation

Researchers are testing FMT in other conditions, especially inflammatory bowel disease.

A 2023 meta-analysis pooled 13 randomized trials of FMT in ulcerative colitis (580 patients). It found that clinical remission was more likely with FMT than with control treatment (risk ratio 1.73), with no significant difference in adverse events. The authors noted that reaching endoscopic remission and keeping people in remission over time remain difficult. [7]

Despite those signals, the AGA guideline recommends **against** conventional FMT for inflammatory bowel disease and irritable bowel syndrome outside clinical trials. [3][4] Interest in FMT for other conditions, including ones tied to the gut–brain connection, is mostly at an early stage. You can browse that work on [Research]({{ '/research/' | relative_url }}).

## Risks and safety considerations

FMT is a transfer of living organisms from another person, so screening is central to its safety.

- **Infection from donor stool.** In 2019 the FDA reported that two immunocompromised adults who received investigational FMT developed invasive infections with a drug-resistant *E. coli*, and one died. Both had received stool from the same donor, which had not been tested for that organism. The FDA called for donor questionnaires that address drug-resistant organism risk and for testing donor stool for these organisms. [6]
- **Do-it-yourself FMT.** Stool from an unscreened donor, such as a friend or family member, does not carry the protections that screened products do. The 2019 events show why screening matters.
- **People with weakened immune systems.** The AGA guideline suggests selective use of conventional FMT in mildly or moderately immunocompromised adults and advises against fecal microbiota–based therapy in severely immunocompromised adults. [3][4]
- **The delivery method has its own risks.** A colonoscopy, for example, carries the usual risks of the procedure and sedation. Short-term digestive symptoms after treatment are commonly reported.

## Open questions

- Which donor features and which microbes are responsible for the benefit?
- What are the best route, dose, and number of treatments, and who should get them?
- How well do the benefits last, and what are the long-term safety effects?
- Can the results in recurrent *C. difficile* be repeated in other conditions, and in which patients?
- How will screening, regulation, and access evolve as more products reach the market?
