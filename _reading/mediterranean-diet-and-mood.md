## Overview

A **Mediterranean diet** is an eating pattern based on the traditional foods of countries around the Mediterranean Sea. It centers on vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes (beans and lentils), nuts, olive oil and oily fish, with little red meat or processed food and sweets and alcohol as occasional extras. [11]

This article covers three questions: how this way of eating relates to gut microbes, whether diet affects how well a fecal transplant works, and what is known about diet and mood. The short answer is that the gut findings are encouraging but mostly observational, and the findings for bipolar disorder are very limited.

## Diet and the gut microbiome

Gut microbes feed on what reaches the colon, especially fiber from plants. When they ferment it, they make **short-chain fatty acids**, compounds that researchers study for effects on the gut lining, inflammation and the gut–brain connection.

- In a study of 153 Italian adults who ate omnivore, vegetarian or vegan diets, people whose eating was high in plant foods in the Mediterranean pattern had higher fecal short-chain fatty acids, more *Prevotella*, and more fiber-degrading bacteria. Those with lower Mediterranean adherence had higher levels of a compound called TMAO in their urine. The study compared people at one point in time, so it can't show that the diet caused the differences. [1]
- In the NU-AGE project, 612 non-frail or pre-frail older adults in five European countries followed a Mediterranean-style diet adapted for older people for 12 months. Higher adherence was linked to shifts in specific bacteria, and the bacteria that increased were associated with lower frailty, better cognitive scores and lower levels of inflammatory markers. The metabolite findings were inferred from the bacteria present rather than measured directly. [2]

Both studies were in general populations, not people with psychiatric conditions.

## Variety: the "30 plants" idea

Many people aiming to feed their gut microbes focus on the **number of different plants** they eat in a week, not just the total amount. The most-cited basis is the American Gut Project, a large citizen-science study. It found that people who ate more than 30 different plant types a week had more diverse gut microbiomes than those who ate 10 or fewer. [3][4]

That is an association. People answered a voluntary diet survey, and the researchers say they don't yet know whether raising microbial diversity would improve health. [4] The number 30 is a useful target, not a threshold with a known effect.

Fruits and vegetables are the easiest way to add variety. Whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, herbs and spices also add different plants.

Some companies sell products designed to add variety. One example is ZOE's Daily30+, a powder blend of more than 30 plant foods that is sprinkled on meals. The maker describes it as a complement to a healthy diet, not a replacement. [14]

It has been tested in a randomized trial published in the *British Journal of Nutrition* in 2026. In that six-week trial, 349 healthy UK adults with low fiber intake took the 30-plant blend, a probiotic, or a bread-crouton control. More bacterial species changed in abundance in the blend group (57, compared with 14 for the control and 4 for the probiotic), and species richness fell slightly. The trial's main pre-specified comparison, whether the species that changed were ones that ZOE's own ranking tool rates as favorable, did not differ significantly between groups. Compared with the control, the blend group reported small improvements in energy, happiness and some digestive symptoms (indigestion, constipation, flatulence and heartburn). The energy gain was similar to the probiotic's, and anxiety ratings did not differ. The authors call these changes small and of uncertain clinical significance, and say the secondary outcomes were exploratory and not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Body weight, blood markers and overall sleep quality did not differ between groups. [17]

Caveats: ZOE funded the trial, and several authors work for ZOE, including its two co-founders, who are also authors. The company sells the product. Participants were recruited from ZOE's mailing list and product waitlist and could not be fully blinded. The trial was short, most participants were women (75%) and White (93%), and it enrolled healthy people rather than people with mood disorders. The recipe has been updated since an earlier version, so the trial may not have tested the current formula. [13] A consumer review also noted that similar variety can be reached more cheaply with everyday foods. [13]

The site mentions it because it is something Monica and Ryan tried, not as an endorsement.

## Diet around fecal transplants

Because a transplant introduces new microbes, researchers are asking whether diet helps them settle in. Two recent reviews looked at this.

- A review of nine human studies, mostly in gut diseases, found that most reported added benefit from combining diet with FMT. For example, one reported remission in 65.7% of ulcerative colitis patients given an anti-inflammatory diet plus transplant, compared with 35.5% on standard therapy. One trial found no benefit from added fiber. The studies were small and short, and several had no transplant-alone comparison. [5]
- A scoping review of 18 papers found that the studies varied widely and gave little detail on diet. Some suggested that diet or fiber improved results, but the authors say diet's effect on transplant success remains unclear. [6]

Nothing here comes from trials of FMT for psychiatric conditions.

## Diet and depression

- In the **SMILES** trial, adults with moderate to severe depression who had seven sessions with a dietitian to move toward a Mediterranean-style diet did better over 12 weeks than a social-support control group. About 32% of the diet group reached remission compared with 8% of the control group. The trial was small, with 56 people providing complete data, and most participants were also receiving other treatment. [7]
- A **meta-analysis** of 16 randomized trials with 45,826 participants found that dietary improvement reduced depressive symptoms (a small effect), with no significant effect on anxiety. Fifteen of the 16 studies were in people without a clinical depression diagnosis, and the trials used different diets, so it can't say which components matter. [8]

## Diet and bipolar disorder

The evidence here is thin.

- A 2024 study of 66 people with bipolar disorder who were stable or mildly depressed found that adherence to a Mediterranean diet was low. Higher adherence was linked to better perceived quality of life. A vegetarian-style eating pattern was linked to lower depression scores and better functioning. The study compared people at one point in time, so it can't show that diet caused those differences. [9]
- A systematic review of Mediterranean diet studies in people with serious mental illness, including bipolar disorder, found 13 studies with inconsistent results. It is available only as a conference abstract and doesn't report bipolar-specific findings. [10]
- A randomized trial comparing a Mediterranean diet with time-restricted eating in adults with bipolar disorder has been designed, with about 300 participants planned. No results have been reported that we could find. [11][12]

We found no completed trial showing that a Mediterranean diet treats bipolar disorder or prevents mood episodes.

## Food and medication safety

Changing your diet is low risk for most people, but large changes can interact with some medicines.

- **Grapefruit** (and related fruits such as Seville oranges and pomelos) can raise levels of certain drugs. The FDA lists some anti-anxiety drugs among those affected and advises asking a provider or pharmacist about your specific medicine. [15]
- **Lithium** levels depend on how much sodium and fluid you take in. One case report described a woman whose lithium level rose without a dose change when her salt intake dropped sharply. [16] If you take lithium, mention major changes in how you eat to your prescriber.

Diet should not replace psychiatric treatment, and psychiatric medication shouldn't be stopped without medical supervision.

## Limitations

- Most findings are observational, so they can't separate diet from other differences between people.
- "Mediterranean diet" is defined and scored differently across studies.
- Trials often combine diet with other changes, such as coaching or exercise.
- Evidence from older adults, people with gut disease or people with depression may not apply to bipolar disorder.

## Key takeaways

- A Mediterranean-style diet with plenty of plant variety is a reasonable, low-risk thing to do alongside treatment, and there are plausible gut-based reasons it could help.
- The evidence is strongest for general health and for depression, weaker for FMT, and thinnest for bipolar disorder.
- Check with your prescriber before making big dietary changes, especially if you take lithium.

## Open questions

- Does a Mediterranean diet change mood episodes in bipolar disorder? The ongoing trial may help answer this.
- Does diet change how well transplanted microbes settle in, and does that affect mood?
- How much does variety matter compared with total fiber?
- Which parts of the diet matter most, and for whom?
